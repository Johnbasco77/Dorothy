After Oppenheimer and Black (2024 Tamil movie), Dorothy is another film that made me look at cinema through a scientific lens. The first two made me think about the complexities of quantum physics. Dorothy made me think about something perhaps even more complex: human identity, gender, and the biases of the human mind. Including my own.

Dorothy is the story of Subbu, who grows up in a family ruled by a cruel, dominating father. He is deeply casteist, believes strongly in male superiority, and uses violence to control his family. His brutality eventually takes the lives of Subbu's mother, sister and sister-in-law, Dorothy.

Growing up in that house, Subbu has another struggle going on silently inside him. From a young age, he begins to realise that he feels like a woman inside. But in a family where even women are not allowed to live freely, he has no courage to say it.

Then Dorothy comes into his life. She understands Subbu in a way nobody else does. One of the most touching scenes for me is when she tells him:

"Your father and brothers suppress the women outside. But you are suppressing the woman inside you."

She gives Subbu something he had probably never felt before: that it is okay to be who he really is.

The journey isn't easy. When Subbu opens up about his gender identity to Karthi, his dear childhood friend, even Karthi finds it hard to accept and starts moving away. That part felt very real to me. Acceptance can be difficult even for people who love us. Eventually, Karthi understands and accepts Subbu as a woman.

And then comes the climax. Subbu is given a new name, "Dorothy", in memory of the sister-in-law who first understood the woman within him and gave him the courage to accept himself. For me, that is what makes the title so meaningful. Dorothy saw the woman inside Subbu before the world could see her. And finally, Subbu becomes Dorothy.

Maestro Ilaiyaraaja's music is the soul of this film, once again proving that his greatness lies not just in composing music, but in making us feel the story.

In one beautiful moment, he re-scores a BGM from his own 38-year-old classic, Veedu. I kept wondering why he picked that particular piece. In Veedu, that BGM plays as an old, fatherly man enjoys the inner beauty of the house being newly constructed. In Dorothy, it plays when Subbu realises the femininity within him.

In both scenes, someone is discovering a beauty from within.

Was that why Ilaiyaraaja chose the same musical soul for both moments? That's only my reading of it. But if so, what brilliance! It felt to me like, alongside director Karthik Subbaraj, the Maestro was telling us the emotion inside that scene.

On my first watch, I questioned the climax. On my second, the climax questioned me.

The first time, I enjoyed the movie and was disappointed by some of the negative reactions to its LGBTQ+ theme. Yet even I felt the director could have avoided the scene where Karthi realises and accepts Subbu, now Dorothy, as a woman and kisses her.

My second watch changed my perspective. I began to understand why Karthik Subbaraj may have chosen a kiss instead of stopping at a comfortable hug. A hug would have been easier for many of us to accept. But perhaps that was exactly the point. Karthi has loved Subbu as his dear friend for years. When he finally accepts Dorothy as the woman she is, the kiss takes that acceptance beyond friendship. He is not merely accepting her gender identity; he is accepting his love for her.

Had the film ended with just a hug, perhaps we would all have walked away comfortably. The kiss forced people like me to confront why that extra step made us uncomfortable in the first place. Maybe it would have taken another decade, or another responsible filmmaker, to ask that uncomfortable question.

And this is the harder part to admit.

In the past, I have had harsh feelings towards gender-diverse people, especially when I came across them at traffic signals and other public places. Watching Dorothy made me reflect on that behaviour and recognise the bias within me. I genuinely regret it. The movie helped me look at them with greater understanding and, more importantly, look within myself without prejudice.

I'm glad I watched Dorothy. It helped me notice the log in my own eye before pointing at the speck in someone else's.

This is also where Dorothy unexpectedly connected with my professional world.

We talk a lot about building unbiased, responsible and ethical AI systems. We worry about biased training data, fairness, guardrails and responsible outcomes. But one thought disturbed me: how can we expect AI to be free from bias when the humans who build and train it still carry biases of their own?

I don't have to look at society for an example. I can start with myself. Some of the discomfort I felt during my first watch came from biases I had accumulated over the years. My second watch made me recognise some of them.

We continuously talk about cleaning and improving the training data of AI. Perhaps, once in a while, we also need to examine our own training data: our upbringing, culture, experiences, assumptions and prejudices.

Because ultimately, Responsible AI cannot only be about responsible machines. It must also be about responsible humans who design, train and govern them.
